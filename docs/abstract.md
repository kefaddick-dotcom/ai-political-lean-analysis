# Abstract

Large language model chatbots have become a routine source of political information. Previous studies using explicit instrument register consistently find a left-of-centre lean, and conversations with opinionated models can shift users' own views. However, ordinary people will ask loosely worded, informal questions that often carry presuppositions and stated opinions. Currently it is unclear that whether the lean measured with explicit instruments appears in everyday use, or which features of everyday language move it. We address this gap in two studies. In Study 1, N = 35 participants across the political spectrum each hold an unscripted conversation with one of five chatbots (ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini, Grok and DeepSeek) about one of six pretested political issues. This is followed by a structured interview on trust, agreement, and perceived partisanship. The interview messages will be blindly coded for speech act, directness, presupposition and evaluative language. In Study 2, we use latent class analysis to derive recurring question types from these conversations and build a preregistered bank of minimal-pair prompts that vary one linguistic feature at a time against a instrument-style baseline. We will submit these queries to the same five chatbots and rate each response on a 7-point liberal-conservative scale. We test whether everyday questions yield non-neutral answers, whether the size of the lean differs between everyday and instrument register, and whether each chatbot returns a consistent position across repeated, reworded queries. Our findings speak to how political audits for AI should be designed and to the growing role of chatbots as everyday political intermediaries.
